Electrons and Photons

Put an electron and a photon in the same box and both have the same allowed wavelengths and momenta, yet the energy levels of one grow as \(n^2\) and of the other as \(n\). The difference comes from a single line: \(E = p^2/2m\) for a massive particle and \(E = pc\) for a photon. That line, in turn, comes from the orders of the time and space derivatives in the wave equations the two obey.

Same box, same momenta, different energies

Trap either one in a box (or a cavity) of length \(L\). The boundary condition is the same, a standing wave fixed at both ends, so the allowed wavelengths and momenta are the same too:

\( \displaystyle \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \)\( \displaystyle p_n = \frac{h}{\lambda_n} = \frac{h}{2L}\,n, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \)

The energies are not:

\( \displaystyle \text{massive particle: } E_n = \frac{p_n^2}{2m} = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}\,n^2, \)\( \displaystyle \text{photon: } E_n = p_n c = \frac{hc}{2L}\,n. \)

The quantization of momentum is identical; everything else follows from how the energy depends on the momentum. (Do not try \(\tfrac12 mv^2\) for a photon: it gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0 \cdot c^2 = 0\). That formula only holds for slow massive particles.)

From wave equation to dispersion relation

A linear wave equation with constant coefficients has plane waves \(\psi = A\,e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}\) as solutions. On a plane wave, derivatives become multiplications,

\( \displaystyle \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \to -i\omega, \qquad \nabla \to i\mathbf{k}, \qquad \nabla^2 \to -k^2, \)

so the differential equation becomes an algebraic relation between \(\omega\) and \(k\), the dispersion relation \(\omega(k)\). With \(E = \hbar\omega\) (Planck–Einstein) and \(p = \hbar k\) (de Broglie), the dispersion relation is the energy–momentum relation \(E(p)\).

The free Schrödinger equation is first order in time and second order in space:

\( \displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi \)\( \displaystyle \Longrightarrow\ \hbar\omega = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} \)\( \displaystyle \Longrightarrow\ E = \frac{p^2}{2m}. \)

In fact the equation was built that way, by substituting \(E \to i\hbar\partial_t\) and \(\mathbf{p} \to -i\hbar\nabla\) into the classical \(E = p^2/2m\): \(E \propto p^2\) is written into its form. Maxwell's equations in vacuum give, after taking the curl of Faraday's law, a wave equation that is second order in both time and space:

\( \displaystyle \nabla^2\mathbf{E} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0 \)\( \displaystyle \Longrightarrow\ \omega = ck \)\( \displaystyle \Longrightarrow\ E = pc. \)

This is the classical \(\lambda f = c\) again, and with \(E = hf\) and \(p = h/\lambda\) it gives \(E = pc = hf\). The rule of thumb: if the time derivative has order \(a\) and the space derivative order \(b\), then \(\omega^a \propto k^b\), so \(E^a \propto p^b\). The condition \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\) makes light transverse, \(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\), so every \(\mathbf{k}\) has two polarizations.

The two relations differ in more than the exponent. A wave crest moves at the phase velocity \(v_{\text{ph}} = \omega/k\), a wave packet at the group velocity \(v_{\text{g}} = d\omega/dk\). For the electron \(v_{\text{ph}} = \hbar k/2m = v/2\) but \(v_{\text{g}} = \hbar k/m = p/m = v\), the classical velocity. For light both are \(c\).

Dispersion: packets that spread

Build a localized packet by adding plane waves with wave numbers around \(k_0\). If every wave travels at the same speed, as light does in vacuum, the packet keeps its shape forever. If the speed depends on the wavelength, as it does for the Schrödinger equation, the components drift apart and the packet spreads. Watch the crests too: for the electron they move at half the speed of the envelope, appearing at its back and vanishing at its front.

One relation, two limits

Both results are limits of the relativistic relation

\( \displaystyle E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4, \)

which, with \(E \to i\hbar\partial_t\) and \(\mathbf{p} \to -i\hbar\nabla\), becomes the Klein–Gordon equation

\( \displaystyle \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2\psi + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\psi = 0. \)

For \(m = 0\) the last term vanishes and this is Maxwell's wave equation: \(E = pc\). For \(m > 0\) and \(p \ll mc\), \(E = mc^2\sqrt{1 + p^2/m^2c^2} \approx mc^2 + p^2/2m\). Peel off the fast oscillation of the rest energy, \(\psi = \phi\,e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}\), and drop the second time derivative of \(\phi\): what is left is exactly the Schrödinger equation \(i\hbar\partial_t\phi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\phi\). The Schrödinger equation is first order in time only because of this nonrelativistic approximation, and that approximation is what produces \(E \propto p^2\). A photon has no rest energy to approximate around, so the equation stays second order in time and \(E \propto p\).

Counting states in a 3D box

In a cube of side \(L\), each direction is quantized, \(k_i = \pi n_i/L\), so \(p = \frac{h}{2L}\sqrt{n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2}\) and

\( \displaystyle \text{particle: } E = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}\left(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2\right), \)\( \displaystyle \text{photon: } E = \frac{hc}{2L}\sqrt{n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2}. \)

Surfaces of constant energy in \((n_x, n_y, n_z)\) space are spheres in both cases, but the radius \(n\) grows as \(E^{1/2}\) for the particle and as \(E\) for the photon. Counting the lattice points in an eighth of a sphere, \(N = \frac18\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}n^3\) (times 2 for the photon's polarizations), with \(V = L^3\):

\( \displaystyle \text{particle: } g(E) = \frac{V}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}, \)\( \displaystyle \text{photon: } g(E) = \frac{V}{\pi^2\hbar^3c^3}\,E^2. \)

For photons, with \(E = hf\), this is \(g(f)\,df = \frac{8\pi Vf^2}{c^3}df\), the mode count in Planck's radiation law. In general, \(E \propto p^s\) in three dimensions gives \(g(E) \propto E^{3/s - 1}\) and a pressure \(P = \frac{s}{3}u\), where \(u\) is the energy density.

A box quantizes the momentum the same way for both, \(p_n = nh/2L\). Whether the levels go as \(n^2\) or \(n\), and whether the density of states goes as \(E^{1/2}\) or \(E^2\), all comes from the dispersion relation set by the ratio of the orders of the time and space derivatives in the wave equation: 1 : 2 for Schrödinger, 2 : 2 for Maxwell.
